Malton sits in the northeast corner of Mississauga, wedged between Toronto
Pearson International Airport and the Brampton city line. Unlike the
lakeside villages further south, Malton's history isn't really about farms
turning slowly into suburbs. It's about an airfield and an aircraft plant
that briefly made this corner of the city one of the most important
industrial sites in the country.

## A short history

In 1937, agents for the Toronto Harbour Commission began buying up farmland
south of the village of Malton to build a new airfield, an alternative to
the cramped Toronto Island Airport downtown. The first aircraft landed at
Malton Airport in August 1938, and Trans-Canada Air Lines, the predecessor
to Air Canada, began using the field the following year.

The Second World War turned Malton into an aircraft manufacturing town
almost overnight. In late 1942, the federal government expropriated
farmland next to the airport to build housing, known ever since as
**Victory Village**, for workers at the new Victory Aircraft plant on the
airfield. The plant's workforce grew from roughly 3,300 in 1942 to more
than 9,500 by 1944, close to a quarter of them women working as riveters,
welders, and inspectors. By the time production wound down in November
1945, Victory Aircraft had built 430 Avro Lancaster bombers, at points
completing nearly one a day.

After the war, the plant passed to A.V. Roe Canada, better known as Avro,
which built the CF-100 fighter and later the CF-105 Avro Arrow, an
advanced supersonic interceptor designed and assembled entirely in Malton.
The Arrow program was cancelled without warning on February 20, 1959, a
day still remembered locally as **Black Friday**, when the federal
government scrapped the program and the existing airframes were cut
apart. Roughly 14,000 workers at the plant lost their jobs that same
afternoon, with estimates of the total job losses across subcontractors
running far higher still. Some of the laid-off engineers went on to work
at NASA in the years that followed.

## Getting there

**Malton GO Station**, on GO Transit's Kitchener line, sits close to the
airport but isn't directly connected to any terminal. Getting from the
station to Pearson's terminals means a short bus ride or a taxi, not a
walk. The dedicated rail link to the airport itself is the separate
**UP Express**, which runs from Toronto's Union Station to a station built
directly at Terminal 1. MiWay buses connect Malton to the rest of
Mississauga. See our
[transit guide](/guides/getting-around-mississauga-transit/) for the
wider picture.

## What's there

- **Westwood Square.** The neighbourhood's main shopping centre, reflecting
  Malton's multicultural population with a mix of South Asian, Caribbean,
  and African grocers and restaurants nearby. Browse our
  [restaurant directory](/directory/restaurants/) for what's currently
  open and highly rated in the area.
- **Malton Community Centre.** A city recreation facility on Morning Star
  Drive, rated 4.3 from over a thousand reviews in our
  [fitness & sports directory](/directory/fitness/), with programming for
  newcomers, youth, adults, and seniors run through Malton Neighbourhood
  Services.
- **Toronto Pearson International Airport.** Still the reason Malton exists
  on the map. The airport sits just south of the neighbourhood, on the
  site of that original 1938 airfield.

## Good to know

- Malton is one of the most ethnically diverse neighbourhoods in
  Mississauga, with a large South Asian population alongside significant
  Caribbean, African, and other immigrant communities, something you'll
  notice quickly in the food and grocery options around Westwood Square.
- Living this close to the airport means noticeable air traffic noise in
  parts of the neighbourhood, worth factoring in if you're considering the
  area for more than a visit.
- The Avro Arrow's cancellation is still a point of local pride and
  frustration alike. Heritage Mississauga's archives cover this era in
  far more depth than fits in this guide, if you want the fuller story.
