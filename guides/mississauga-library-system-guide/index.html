Mississauga's Library System: A Guide to Every Branch

Published October 18, 2026 · Mississauga Insider

Mississauga runs one of the larger municipal library systems in the Greater Toronto Area: the Hazel McCallion Central Library downtown, plus a network of smaller neighbourhood branches spread across the city. Here’s how the system is organized and what’s worth knowing before you go.

The central library and the neighbourhood branches

The Hazel McCallion Central Library sits in City Centre, close to Celebration Square and Square One, and functions as the system’s flagship location, with the largest collection and the widest range of programs and study space.

Around it, the city runs a set of smaller neighbourhood branches, each serving its own part of Mississauga. Several of them sit in neighbourhoods we’ve already covered in our neighbourhood guides, including branches serving Clarkson, Streetsville, Malton, Lakeview, and Meadowvale, plus the Port Credit branch, which closed for several years before reopening to the public in 2023. Rather than list every address and set of hours here (branches occasionally adjust their schedules), the most reliable way to find your nearest branch and its current hours is through the library’s own locations page, linked from the city’s website.

What a library card actually costs

A library card is free if you live in Mississauga, work there, study there, or own property there. Resident cards are renewed roughly every two years rather than being a one-time sign-up. If none of those apply to you, the system charges a yearly non-resident fee rather than turning you away.

If you just want digital access, there’s a quicker option: a virtual card that can be issued instantly online, which unlocks e-books, audiobooks, and other digital collections without a trip to a branch. Getting a physical card still requires showing ID with your current name and address, since that’s what lets you borrow from the shelves and use in-branch services.

More than just books

Beyond borrowing, branches across the system generally offer printing, photocopying, and scanning, along with public computers and Wi-Fi. Many also run regular programming, from children’s storytime to seasonal events and drop-in programs for newcomers, which is worth checking for regardless of which branch is closest to you.

Practical takeaway

If you’re new to the city, your closest branch is probably in whichever neighbourhood you live or work in; check our neighbourhood guides for area-specific context, and use the directory if you’re looking for other community resources nearby. A free resident card and a short trip to confirm current hours at your local branch is really all it takes to get started.
